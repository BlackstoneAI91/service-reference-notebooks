## Why a Wishlist Note Needs to Be Short and Specific

A donor-facing wishlist note has one job: help a volunteer decide what to buy, or whether to buy at all, without needing to ask a follow-up question. That is harder than it sounds, because the person reading the note is usually not the person who wrote it. They may be reading on a phone between other tasks, and they may never have handled the item before.

The temptation is to write a long explanation of the group's work. Resist it. The note should open with the request itself, then add only the details a buyer needs to act correctly. Everything else belongs in a separate message or a conversation.

A useful structure for a short note is four lines:

1. What the organiser has requested, described plainly.
2. Who will check options before ordering, and how to reach that person.
3. How to mark an item as pledged so two people do not buy the same thing.
4. A short line on what the group will and will not accept.

## Start With the Item, Not the Story

The first line of the note should name the kind of item wanted, in the words a shopper would actually use. If the group needs a walking harness, say so, and say what it is for in one clause. For example: "We are looking for a dog harness for our weekend walking volunteers."

Then add the constraints that change what a buyer would pick. Useful constraints include:

- The size range or weight band the group needs, described in general terms rather than as fitting instructions.
- Whether the group wants a new item or will accept a clean second-hand one.
- Whether a particular style is required, such as a harness rather than a collar and lead set.
- Whether the group can store spares, which affects how many of one item is useful.

Avoid writing a product recommendation. The note should describe a need, not endorse a brand. If the organiser has a specific listing in mind, say that the organiser will confirm the exact item before anyone orders, rather than pasting a link into a public note.

This is also where a phrase like dog harness online can appear naturally, because it describes how a volunteer might search. A sentence such as "Some volunteers prefer to compare dog harness online listings before deciding" reads as context, not as an instruction to buy from a particular seller.

## Name the Person Who Checks Options Before Ordering

The second line answers the question every careful donor asks: who decides? Put a role, not a personal name, in the public note, and give a single route for questions. For example: "Our volunteer coordinator will check options before any order is placed. Please message the coordinator before buying so we can confirm the item is still needed."

This line does three things at once. It tells the reader that the group is organised, it prevents duplicate purchases, and it gives the reader a clear next step if they are unsure. It also protects the group from receiving items it cannot use.

Keep the contact route generic in the public note, such as the group's main messaging channel. Personal phone numbers and home addresses should not appear in a donor-facing document. If a volunteer needs to share details, that belongs in a private reply.

A short note on timing helps too. Something like "We review the list at the end of each month" tells the reader when to expect an answer, without promising a response time the group cannot keep.

## A Worked Example of a Short Donor Note

Here is a fictional example of how the four lines fit together. It is a template, not a real appeal, and the group described does not exist.

"Thank you for offering to help with our dog accessory wishlist.

What we need: a dog harness suitable for medium-sized dogs, for our weekend walking volunteers. New items only, please. We can store one spare.

Who checks options: our volunteer coordinator will compare options before any order is placed. Some volunteers like to look at dog harness online listings first, which is fine, but please message the coordinator before buying so we can confirm the item is still needed.

How to mark a pledge: reply to this note with the words PLEDGED and the item name. The coordinator will update the list and confirm. If you see PLEDGED next to an item, please choose something else.

What we cannot accept: items that are damaged, or that need repair before use. If you are unsure, ask first."

Notice what the example does not do. It does not name a shop, quote a price, recommend a brand, or give fitting directions. It describes a need and a process. That keeps the note useful to a donor without turning it into an advertisement or a set of instructions the group cannot stand behind.

## How to Mark a Pledged Item So Two People Do Not Buy It

Duplicate purchases are the most common failure in a small wishlist, and they are almost always a process problem rather than a donor problem. Two people read the same note, both decide to help, and neither knows the other exists.

The simplest fix is a visible status next to each item. A short list with three states works well:

- OPEN: no one has committed yet.
- PLEDGED: someone has said they will buy it, and the coordinator has confirmed.
- RECEIVED: the item has arrived and been checked.

Ask donors to use one exact word, such as PLEDGED, in their reply. A single keyword is easier to scan than a paragraph, and it reduces the chance that a well-meaning message is missed. The coordinator should then update the shared list and reply to confirm, so the donor knows the pledge has been recorded.

Two practical rules make this work. First, only the coordinator changes the status, so the list has one source of truth. Second, a pledge has an expiry, such as two weeks, after which the item returns to OPEN. That prevents an item sitting as PLEDGED forever because someone changed their mind quietly.

If the group uses a shared document, keep the status column at the left edge where it is seen first. If the group uses a messaging thread, pin the current list so new readers do not scroll past an old version.

## What to Leave Out of a Public Wishlist Note

A donor note is a public document, even when it is shared in a small group. Some details should stay out of it.

Leave out personal contact details, home addresses and anything that identifies a volunteer individually. Leave out internal arrangements, such as who stores what or how items are transported. Leave out prices and shop names, because they date quickly and can read as an endorsement. Leave out fitting directions and product recommendations, since the group is not in a position to give them and a wrong instruction could cause harm.

It is also worth being clear about what the group will not accept. A short line on this saves everyone time. If the group cannot store large items, say so. If it cannot accept used bedding, say so. A wishlist that lists only what is wanted, and never what is not, invites well-meant donations the group has to turn away.

Finally, keep the note honest about what the group can confirm. If the coordinator has not yet checked a listing, the note should say the item is still being reviewed, not that it has been approved.

## A Short Checklist Before You Publish the Note

Before the note goes out, run through a short checklist. This is an author heuristic, not a tested standard, so adapt it to the group's size and habits.

- Does the first line name the item and the purpose?
- Does the note say who checks options before ordering, and how to reach them?
- Is there one exact word for marking a pledge, and one person who updates the status?
- Does a pledge expire, and is the expiry stated?
- Are personal contact details, prices, shop names and fitting directions absent?
- Does the note say what the group will not accept?
- If a Petpalz Malaysia page is shared, is it described as a guide rather than a shop?
- Is the affiliate disclosure present on any page that carries affiliate links?

A note that passes these checks is short enough to read on a phone and specific enough to act on. That is the whole aim: fewer questions, fewer duplicates, and a clearer path from a donor's good intention to an item the group can actually use.
