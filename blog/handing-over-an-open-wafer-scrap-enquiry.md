## Why a Contact Change Is a Risk Point for an Open Enquiry

Administrative contacts move between desks, teams and companies. When the person who opened a wafer scrap collection enquiry leaves or changes role, the enquiry does not close with them. It stays open in a mailbox, a chat thread or a spreadsheet, and the next person inherits it without the context that made it make sense.

The risk is not that the enquiry disappears. The risk is that it gets misread. A tentative collection date mentioned in an early reply can look, to a fresh reader, like a confirmed appointment. A question the supplier asked and never had answered can look like a settled point. A request the buyer sent can look like an acceptance the supplier gave.

This guide is written for a fictional Singapore procurement team handling that handover. It sets out a simple way to record the state of an open enquiry so the incoming administrative contact can pick it up accurately. It does not cover material acceptance criteria, handling, transport, disposal, costs or regulatory requirements, and it offers no advice on those points. Those belong to the parties and specialists responsible for them.

## Separate the Request From the Acceptance

The single most useful habit in a handover is to keep two things apart: what your side asked for, and what the supplier agreed to. In an open enquiry these are often mixed in the same thread, and a new reader can easily merge them.

A request is something your team sent. It might be a question about whether a collection can be arranged, a proposed window, or a request for information. Sending it does not commit the supplier to anything.

An acceptance is something the supplier stated. It is a clear confirmation that a specific arrangement is agreed. Anything short of that, including a friendly reply, an expression of interest or a suggested date, is not an acceptance.

A tentative date is the most common source of confusion. If a supplier writes that a date might work, or proposes a date subject to confirmation, the enquiry is still open. The incoming contact should be told, in plain words, that no date is confirmed.

A practical test for the handover note: for each item in the thread, ask who said it and what it commits. If the answer is unclear, mark it as outstanding rather than deciding on the new contact's behalf. The incoming person can then ask the supplier directly instead of relying on a guess made during the handover.

## A Simple Status Register for the Handover

A short register is enough. It does not need software or a template library. Three columns carry most of the value: the source message, the outstanding supplier question, and the owner.

Source message. Record where the item came from, in a way the next person can find again. A date, a channel and a one-line description are usually sufficient. The point is traceability, not a full transcript. If the incoming contact can open the original message, they can check the wording themselves.

Outstanding supplier question. Record what is still unanswered, phrased as a question. For example: has the supplier confirmed whether the proposed window is workable? Has the supplier responded to the last message? Writing it as a question makes it obvious that the matter is open.

Owner. Record who is responsible for the next step. After a contact change, this is often the incoming administrative contact, but it may be a colleague who holds the technical relationship. Naming one owner prevents the enquiry from sitting in a shared inbox with everyone assuming someone else has it.

Two optional columns help without adding much effort. Status can be a single word such as open, awaiting supplier or awaiting us. Last reviewed can be a date, so the next reader knows how current the register is.

Keep the register free of anything confidential or personal beyond what the handover needs. A name, a role and a work contact route are usually enough. Do not copy private contact details into a wider document than necessary.

## Writing the Handover Note Itself

The register records state. The handover note explains it. A short note of a few paragraphs is usually more useful than a long one, because the incoming contact is more likely to read it.

Start with one sentence on what the enquiry is and where it stands. For example: an open enquiry about a possible collection, no date confirmed, last message from our side awaiting a reply.

Then list what is confirmed and what is not. Be explicit about tentative dates. If a date was mentioned but not agreed, say so. If the supplier asked a question that has not been answered, say so and say who should answer it.

Then point to the source messages. The note should tell the reader where to look, not reproduce the thread. This keeps the note short and keeps the original wording available for checking.

Finally, state the next action and its owner. One action is usually enough. If there are several, order them so the reader knows what comes first.

Avoid two habits that cause trouble. The first is summarising a tentative date as if it were firm. The second is describing a request as if it were agreed. Both read smoothly and both mislead. A handover note that says plainly what is open is more useful than one that sounds finished.

## What the Incoming Contact Should Check First

The incoming administrative contact has a short list of checks before doing anything else. These are author heuristics, not a formal procedure, and a team can adjust them to fit its own practice.

First, confirm the enquiry is genuinely open. Read the last message from each side and check whether anything has since closed it.

Second, confirm the status of any date. If a date appears anywhere in the thread, check whether the supplier confirmed it or merely mentioned it. Treat anything short of a clear confirmation as tentative.

Third, identify the outstanding question. If the supplier asked something and no answer is recorded, that is the first thing to resolve.

Fourth, confirm the owner. If the register names someone else, check with them before acting.

Fifth, check that the register and the note agree. If they differ, the source messages decide.

A suggested rhythm, offered as an example rather than a rule, is to review the register at the start of the week and again before any contact with the supplier. The aim is simply that no one acts on a stale reading of the thread.

## Keeping the Register Useful Over Time

A register written once and never updated becomes a trap. The incoming contact should update it whenever something changes, and should date each update so the next reader knows how fresh it is.

When a supplier answers an outstanding question, move the item from open to closed and record the answer in one line. When a new question arises, add it. When ownership changes again, update the owner column rather than starting a new register.

Retire the register when the enquiry closes, and keep a short note of how it closed. That record helps if a similar enquiry comes up later, and it stops an old open item from being mistaken for a live one.

A useful discipline is to keep the register separate from any working document that contains confidential or commercial detail. The register is a status tool for administrative handover, not a place for sensitive information.

## A Worked Example of the Register in Use

The following example is fictional and is included only to show the shape of the register. It does not describe a real enquiry, a real supplier or a real date.

Suppose a procurement coordinator sent a message asking whether a collection could be arranged in a particular week. The supplier replied that the week might be possible but did not confirm it. The coordinator then changed role.

The register might hold three rows. Row one: source message, the coordinator's original request, dated; outstanding supplier question, has the supplier confirmed the week is workable; owner, incoming administrative contact. Row two: source message, the supplier's reply, dated; outstanding supplier question, none, but the reply is not a confirmation; owner, incoming administrative contact. Row three: source message, the coordinator's last message, dated; outstanding supplier question, has the supplier responded; owner, incoming administrative contact.

The handover note would then say, in a few sentences, that the enquiry is open, that no date is confirmed, that the supplier's reply mentioned a week without agreeing to it, and that the next action is to ask the supplier to confirm or propose an alternative.

That is the whole method. It is not elaborate, and it does not require special tools. Its value is that it stops a tentative date from hardening into a confirmed one during a contact change, and it gives the incoming person a clear place to start.
