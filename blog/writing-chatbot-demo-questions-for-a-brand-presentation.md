## Why the Demo Questions Shape the Whole Brief

A chatbot demo is often the first time a project team sees its own service described back to it in a customer's words. The questions chosen for that demo quietly set the scope for everything that follows: what the assistant is expected to answer, which pages it draws on, and where a human must step in. If the sample questions are vague, the brief inherits that vagueness.

For a Malaysian service business with a public website, the safest starting point is information the company already publishes: service names, opening hours, location, how to request a quotation, and which enquiries need a person. A demo built only on public information is easy to review, easy to correct, and does not require anyone to hand over customer records or internal documents just to see how the assistant behaves.

This is also where an AI agency earns its keep. A good partner will ask you to supply the questions rather than inventing them, because the questions reveal what your customers actually struggle to find. ## Write Questions the Way Customers Actually Ask

The most useful demo questions sound slightly untidy. A customer does not type "please enumerate your service offerings"; they type "do you do aircond servicing in Petaling Jaya?" or "can I walk in on Saturday?" Collect the phrasing from your own enquiry log, WhatsApp messages, or the questions staff answer on the phone, then rewrite them so no personal details remain.

Aim for a spread rather than a list of near-duplicates. A practical set might include: one question about what the business does, one about a specific service, one about location or coverage, one about price or quotation process, one about timing, and one that the business genuinely cannot answer without a person. That last question is often the most valuable, because it shows how the assistant should hand over.

Label each question with the intent behind it, for example "service scope", "location", "quotation" or "escalation". This labelling helps a software application development company or chatbot builder map questions to pages and to decision points. It also gives reviewers a shared vocabulary when they disagree about whether an answer was acceptable. Keep the list short enough to review in one sitting; a dozen well-chosen questions usually teaches more than fifty overlapping ones.

## Keep the Demo Inside Public Information

A demo question set should be answerable from material you are willing to publish. That means service descriptions, general pricing guidance if you publish it, location and contact routes, and policy summaries that already appear on the website. It should not require the assistant to look up an individual's account, quote a private contract, or reveal internal procedures.

When you draft the brief, add a short note beside each question stating the source it should draw on, such as a specific service page or a published FAQ. If no public source exists, mark the question as "needs source" rather than letting the demo improvise. This keeps the exercise honest and gives the builder a clear instruction: answer from approved material, or say it cannot answer and offer a route to a person.

This discipline matters for regulated or sensitive enquiries too. A general statement that the assistant should escalate certain topics is fine; detailed instructions about how staff should handle them belong in an internal document, not in a public demo brief. The goal at this stage is to test whether the assistant can stay within the boundary you have drawn, not to test how much it knows.

## Label Every Illustrative Answer Clearly

Once the questions exist, someone will draft sample answers to show what the finished conversation might look like. These drafts are illustrations, not commitments. Label them plainly, for example "Illustrative answer for discussion only — not approved customer wording", and keep them in a separate section of the brief from the questions themselves.

A useful pattern is a three-column table: the customer question, the illustrative answer, and a note on what still needs a decision. The third column is where the real work happens. It might say "confirm whether weekend appointments are offered", "decide whether to mention the second branch", or "agree the handover wording when a quotation is requested". These notes become the action list for the next meeting.

Avoid presenting illustrative answers as tested results. A demo conversation shows how a flow might read; it does not show that customers will find it helpful, that enquiries will increase, or that the assistant will behave the same way once connected to live content. Keeping that distinction visible protects the project from promises nobody can keep and makes later review far easier.

## Record What Still Needs a Decision

A brief that only lists questions and sample answers is incomplete. Add a decisions log with three fields: the decision needed, the person who owns it, and the date it was raised. Typical entries include which services the assistant may discuss, whether it may quote any prices, how it should refer to branches, and what it should say when it does not know.

Group these decisions by reader intent rather than by department. Questions about service scope, location, pricing and escalation each tend to need a different owner, and grouping them this way makes the review faster. Where a decision depends on content that does not yet exist, record that as a content task rather than leaving the question unanswered in the demo.

It also helps to state explicitly what the demo does not cover. Access to internal systems, customer records, payment flows and staff procedures can be discussed later as separate scopes. Writing that boundary into the brief prevents the demo from quietly expanding into a system design exercise before anyone has agreed the basics.

## Review the Set Before It Goes Into the Brief

Before the question set is attached to the project brief, run a short review with the people who answer customer enquiries. Read each question aloud and ask whether a real customer would phrase it that way, whether the answer relies only on public information, and whether the illustrative answer is clearly marked as a draft. This takes less time than a full workshop and catches most of the obvious problems.

A simple checklist helps: every question has a stated intent; every question has a public source or a "needs source" note; every illustrative answer carries a draft label; every open point appears in the decisions log; and no personal or internal details appear anywhere in the document. Treat the checklist as an author's heuristic rather than a certified standard, and adjust it to suit the project.
