## Why a Shared Cover Note Beats Two Separate Enquiries

When a property owner and a commercial tenant both have questions about an auto gate installation, the instinct is often to send two enquiries. That creates a paperwork problem: two reference numbers, two sets of replies, and no single record of what was asked or agreed. A shared cover note solves this by putting every question in one document, clearly labelled with who raised it.

The cover note is not a decision document. It is a routing document. Its job is to show the reader, at a glance, which questions came from the owner, which came from the tenant, and which need a written answer before anything moves forward. Keep it short enough to read in one sitting, and structured enough that a reply can quote it line by line.

A useful habit is to give each question a short reference, such as O1 for owner questions and T1 for tenant questions. This makes later correspondence easier to follow without repeating the full question each time. It also makes it obvious when a reply has skipped something.

## Separating Who Asked From Who Approves

The most common confusion in a joint enquiry is treating the person who asked a question as the person who can approve the answer. These are different roles and should be labelled separately.

Use three simple columns in your cover note: Raised by, Question, and Approval needed from. A tenant's facilities coordinator may raise a question about how a written change would be handled, while the owner's representative is the one who signs off on any variation to the agreed scope. Writing both names against the question removes guesswork later.

Where a question touches shared areas or shared cost, note that explicitly. For example, a question about who receives copies of revised paperwork might need both parties to agree the distribution list. That is an administrative point, not a technical one, and it belongs in the cover note.

Avoid the temptation to merge two people's questions into one line to save space. If the owner and tenant ask similar things for different reasons, keep them separate and cross-reference them. A merged line is harder to answer and harder to audit.

## Writing Questions That Can Be Answered in Writing

A question that cannot be answered in writing is not ready for the cover note. Before sending, test each line by asking whether a reasonable reply could be a short paragraph or a yes or no with a condition.

Weak lines invite vague replies. A line such as "what about the gate?" gives the reader nothing to work with. A stronger line names the document, the stage or the decision it relates to, for example: "Please confirm which party is named as the contact for written variations after the enquiry stage." That is answerable.

Group questions by theme rather than by person. Administrative themes might include documentation, approval sequence, record keeping and communication channels. Within each theme, keep the owner and tenant labels visible so the reader can still see who raised what.

Where a question depends on an earlier answer, say so. A short note such as "this depends on the reply to O2" prevents the reader from answering out of order and creating contradictions.

## Recording Responses in One Place

A cover note is only half the record. The other half is the response log. Decide before sending where replies will be stored and who can edit that record.

A simple response log has one row per question reference, with columns for the date received, the responder, the answer in brief, and any follow-up needed. Keep it in a shared location that both owner and tenant representatives can read, even if only one person is allowed to edit it.

When a reply arrives by message or email, copy the substance into the log rather than relying on the original thread. Threads get forwarded, trimmed and lost. The log is the durable version.

Mark questions as open, answered or superseded. A superseded question is one that a later answer has replaced; leaving it visible but marked prevents someone from acting on an old reply.

## Handling a Written Change Request

At some point a question turns into a change request. The cover note should say in advance how that will be handled, so nobody is surprised when it happens.

A written change request should name the original question reference, state what is being changed, and identify who is authorised to approve it on each side. If the owner and tenant have different approval limits, record both. A change approved by one party but not the other is a dispute waiting to happen.

Keep change requests in a separate numbered series, such as C1, C2, so they are not confused with the original questions. Link each change back to the question it grew from.

Where a change affects cost or scope, the cover note can state that no change takes effect until both named approvers have confirmed in writing. That is a procedural statement, not legal advice, and parties should take their own advice on contract terms.

## A Short Pre-Send Checklist

Before the shared cover note goes out, run through a short checklist. This is an author heuristic, not a researched standard, but it catches the usual gaps.

First, every question has a reference and a named raiser. Second, every question has a named approver or a note saying approval is not yet assigned. Third, no question merges two people's concerns into one line. Fourth, dependent questions point to the reference they depend on. Fifth, the response log location is stated in the cover note itself. Sixth, the change-request route is described in one or two sentences.

A seventh check is tone. The cover note should read as a shared administrative document, not as one party briefing against the other. Neutral wording such as "the owner's representative asked" and "the tenant's coordinator asked" keeps it balanced.

Finally, decide who sends it. One sender with both parties copied is cleaner than two parallel messages. Note the sender and the date in the log so the record starts correctly.
