## What the Brief Is For and Who Owns It

A branch campaign brief is the document that stops a Kuala Lumpur launch from becoming a set of disconnected tasks. It answers one question before any promotion goes live: when someone responds to the campaign, who is responsible for the next step, and how will everyone know it happened?

The brief should name a single owner for the local page and a single owner for the enquiry desk. These can be the same person in a small firm, but the brief should say so explicitly rather than leaving it implied. Write the owner's role, not just a name, so the brief survives staff changes. Add a deputy for each role and the dates they take over.

Keep the brief short enough to be read in one sitting. A useful structure is four parts: the page, the channel copy, the routing rules, and the rehearsal. Each part gets a named reviewer and a sign-off date. Anything not signed off stays out of the campaign.

## Part One: The Local Page Brief

The local page is where the campaign sends people, so the brief must describe it precisely. Start with the page's job in one sentence. For a fictional example, take a Malaysian commercial cleaning company opening a Kuala Lumpur branch. The page's job might be: let a facilities manager in Klang Valley confirm the branch serves their area and start an enquiry without calling a Sarawak number.

Record the following in the brief:

- The page's primary query and two or three supporting phrases, written as a person would type them.
- The services the branch actually delivers, checked against the head office list. Do not list a service the branch cannot staff.
- The service area, stated as areas or postcodes the branch covers, not a vague regional claim.
- The enquiry options: form, phone, WhatsApp, or all three, with the number and hours the branch will answer.
- Who approves the final copy, and who checks that the address and hours match the branch's own records.

If the branch page is being built as part of a wider site refresh, the brief should say whether the page inherits the main site's design or gets its own treatment. A web design KL engagement usually covers this decision early, because a branch page that looks unrelated to the main site confuses visitors who arrive from a shared campaign.

One rule worth writing into the brief: the page must not promise response times the desk cannot meet. If the desk answers within one working day, the page says one working day.

## Part Two: Channel Copy and the Claims It May Make

Campaign copy is where branch launches most often drift from what the business can support. The brief should list every channel in use, the person who writes each piece, and the claims each piece is allowed to make.

For the fictional cleaning company, the channel list might be: a search ad group for Kuala Lumpur office cleaning, two social posts announcing the branch, a listing update on the company's business profile, and a short note in the next customer email. Each entry in the brief records the channel, the owner, the draft date, the approval date, and the destination page.

Claims deserve their own line. The brief should state which claims are approved and which are forbidden. Approved might include the branch opening date, the services offered, and the areas served. Forbidden might include response-time promises, discount language, or any statement about being the largest or best. If a claim is not in the approved list, the writer asks rather than invents.

Where social channels carry the announcement, the copy and the reply plan belong together. A social media marketing agency working on the launch would normally want the branch's reply owner named in the brief, because a post that draws questions with no one assigned to answer them creates the wrong first impression.

Keep a single copy of the approved claims list and link every channel to it. When the list changes, the brief records the change and the date.

## Part Three: Response Routing for the Enquiry Desk

The enquiry desk is the part of the campaign that customers actually experience. The brief should describe routing as a set of rules, not a hope.

Write the rules in a table with four columns: enquiry source, first responder, backup responder, and expected first reply. For the fictional cleaning company, the rules might read:

- Form on the branch page: branch coordinator, branch manager, same working day.
- WhatsApp to the branch number: branch coordinator, branch manager, within two working hours during office hours.
- Phone call outside office hours: voicemail with a call-back promise, branch manager next morning.
- Social media message: marketing assistant, branch coordinator, same working day.

Add what happens when the desk is full. A simple overflow rule, such as forwarding to the head office queue with a note that the enquiry is for Kuala Lumpur, prevents enquiries from sitting unanswered during a busy week.

Record how enquiries are logged. If the business already uses a CRM, the brief names the fields the desk must fill: source, branch, service requested, and outcome. If there is no CRM, the brief names the shared sheet and who reviews it weekly.

This is also where automation earns its place. A workflow that routes form submissions to the right queue and sends an acknowledgement to the enquirer is a modest build, but it must be tested with the branch's real hours before launch. ## Part Four: The Rehearsal Before Launch

A rehearsal is the cheapest way to find out that the brief is wrong. Schedule it at least a week before the campaign goes live, and run it with the people who will actually answer enquiries, not only the managers who wrote the brief.

A workable rehearsal has five steps:

1\. Send a test enquiry through every channel listed in the brief, from an external phone and email account.
2\. Time how long each acknowledgement takes and compare it with the expected first reply in the routing table.
3\. Check that the reply names the Kuala Lumpur branch and gives the correct address and hours.
4\. Ask the responder to log the test enquiry the way a real one would be logged, then check the record.
5\. Repeat one channel with a deliberately awkward enquiry, such as a request for a service the branch does not offer, and confirm the responder knows what to say.

Record the results in the brief itself. Each channel gets a pass or a fix, the person responsible for the fix, and a re-test date. A channel that fails twice moves out of the launch plan until it passes.

The rehearsal also tests the page. Open it on a phone, submit the form, and confirm the confirmation message tells the enquirer what happens next. If the page and the desk disagree about hours or numbers, fix the page first.

## Sign-Off, Review and What to Keep After Launch

The brief ends with a sign-off block. It should list the page owner, the desk owner, the copy approver, and the person who authorised the campaign budget. Each signs and dates. A brief signed by only one person is a plan, not an agreement.

Set a review date for two weeks after launch and another at eight weeks. At each review, the team checks three things: whether enquiries arrived through the channels the brief expected, whether the routing rules held during busy periods, and whether any copy claim needs correcting. Record the answers in the brief rather than in a separate report, so the document stays the single reference.

Keep the brief after the campaign ends. It becomes the starting point for the next branch launch and a record of what the desk learned. If the business later adds a second Kuala Lumpur location or a service line, the routing table and claims list can be extended rather than rewritten.

One caution: a brief is not a guarantee of results. It is a record of decisions. The value comes from making those decisions before customers arrive, and from reviewing them honestly afterwards.
