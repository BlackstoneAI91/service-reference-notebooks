## Why an Altered Garment Needs a Clearer Enquiry

An altered garment carries two sets of information that often get mixed up in a cleaning enquiry. The first is the manufacturer's care label, which describes the original fabric and construction. The second is the tailor's note, which records what was changed after the garment left the factory. When you send a garment for dry cleaning, the cleaning team needs both, kept separate and clearly labelled. Blurring them can lead to the wrong process being chosen or a question being missed.

Consider a fictional Sarawak shopper we will call Mei. She has two altered garments: a lined dress with a shortened hem and a jacket with a replaced lining. On the dress, the original label may still say one thing about the fabric, while the tailor's note records a new stitch line and a different hem finish. On the jacket, the replacement lining may not match the original label at all. If Mei writes only "please dry clean" and drops the items off, the counter staff have to guess which note applies to which part of the garment.

A clear enquiry does not need to be long. It needs to be organised. The aim is to help the cleaning team understand what is original, what was changed, and where the changes sit. That way, the dry cleaning service can review the garment with the right questions in mind. ## Source Labels: Manufacturer Care Label vs Tailor's Note

The manufacturer's care label is attached by the maker and usually covers fibre content and recommended care. Treat it as the starting point, not the whole story. If the garment has been altered, the label may no longer describe the current state of the item. For example, a hem that was shortened may now have a different seam allowance, and a replaced lining may be a different fabric from the original.

The tailor's note is the record of what changed. It might be a handwritten slip, a printed job sheet, or a message from the tailor. It can include details such as "hem shortened by 4 cm", "lining replaced with polyester", or "buttons reattached". These details matter because they tell the cleaning team where to look and what to check before cleaning.

In your enquiry, list each source separately. Write "Manufacturer label says: ..." and "Tailor's note says: ..." as two distinct lines. If you do not have the tailor's note, say so plainly rather than filling the gap with a guess. If the label is missing or unreadable, state that too. The cleaning team can then decide what additional information they need. This approach keeps the enquiry honest and gives the dry cleaning service a clear basis for its review.

## A Side-by-Side Worksheet for Two Altered Garments

A simple worksheet helps you compare two altered garments without mixing their details. Use one column per garment and one row per question. Below is an example layout that Mei could adapt. It is a suggested format, not a formal document required by any cleaner.

Row 1: Garment name. Dress / Jacket.
Row 2: Manufacturer label present? Yes or no. If yes, copy the care wording exactly.
Row 3: Tailor's note present? Yes or no. If yes, copy the alteration details exactly.
Row 4: Where is the alteration? For the dress, the hem. For the jacket, the lining.
Row 5: Any visible marks or stains? Describe location and appearance without guessing the cause.
Row 6: Any loose threads, missing buttons or open seams? Note them.
Row 7: What do you want the cleaner to know first? One short sentence per garment.

Keep the worksheet to one page if possible. The point is not to create paperwork for its own sake but to make sure each garment's information stays with that garment. When you send the enquiry, you can attach a photo of each label and each tailor's note, or type the text into the message. If you are dealing with several items, a worksheet per garment or per pair prevents details from drifting between them.

## The Exact Short Message You Can Adapt

A short, structured message is often easier to read than a long paragraph. Here is a template that Mei could adapt. Replace the bracketed parts with your own details. Do not add claims about the fabric or the stain that you cannot support.

Subject: Dry cleaning enquiry for two altered garments

Hello, I would like to enquire about dry cleaning for two garments.

Garment 1: [dress]. Manufacturer label says: [copy wording]. Tailor's note says: [copy wording]. The alteration is at [hem]. Visible marks: [describe or write none].

Garment 2: [jacket]. Manufacturer label says: [copy wording or write missing]. Tailor's note says: [copy wording]. The alteration is at [lining]. Visible marks: [describe or write none].

I have photos of both labels and both tailor's notes and can send them if useful. Please let me know what else you need to review the garments.

Thank you.

This message does four things. It separates the two garments, separates the two sources of information, states what is visible, and offers photos. It does not ask for a diagnosis or a promise of results. It simply gives the cleaning team a clear starting point. You can use the same structure for a single garment by removing the second block. If you are enquiring on behalf of a household or a venue, add one line saying who you are and how many items are involved, without naming any client.

## What to Leave Out and What to Confirm Before Sending

Some details are better left out of the first enquiry. Avoid telling the cleaner what caused a stain unless you saw it happen. Avoid describing a fabric as "delicate" or "strong" unless that wording comes from a label or a tailor's note. Avoid asking for a specific treatment by name. These additions can mislead more than they help.

Before sending, confirm three things. First, check that each garment's label and note are matched correctly. Second, check that your contact details and preferred branch or pickup arrangement are stated if relevant. Third, check that you have not included information about another person without their consent. If you are a venue manager or accommodation operator, describe the item and the alteration, not the guest.

## After the Enquiry: Keeping the Record Useful

Once the enquiry is sent, keep a copy of what you sent and any reply you receive. If the cleaning team asks for more information, add it to the same worksheet rather than starting a new note. This keeps the garment's history in one place. If you are handling several items over time, a simple folder or spreadsheet with one row per garment can be enough.

When the garment is returned, check it against the worksheet. Note any difference between what you described and what you received. If something needs clarification, refer back to the original message rather than describing the garment from memory. This is especially useful for altered garments, where the tailor's note may be the only record of what changed.
