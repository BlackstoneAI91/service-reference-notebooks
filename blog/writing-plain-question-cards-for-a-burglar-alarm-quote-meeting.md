## Why a Question Card Set Beats a Long Agenda

A quotation meeting for a burglary alarm system often mixes technical terms, administrative details and commercial numbers in one conversation. The person holding the quotation may use words like zone, panel, siren and handover without explaining what each means for the building. A long agenda helps the chair keep time, but it does not help a committee member who needs to ask a simple question in plain language.

A question card set is a small stack of cards, each carrying one or two questions written in everyday English. The cards are printed before the meeting and handed to attendees. Each card has a blank space for the written answer, the name of the person who answered, and the date. The aim is not to test the supplier. The aim is to leave the meeting with a record that someone who was absent can read and understand.

For a fictional Sarawak committee reviewing an ordinary alarm quotation, six to ten cards are usually enough. Keep each card focused on one theme: what is included, what happens at handover, what the committee must prepare, and what the quotation does not cover. The cards should be written for the reader, not for the engineer. If a card needs a glossary to be understood, rewrite the card.

## Choosing the Terms That Need a Plain-Language Card

The first task is to list the words in the quotation that a non-technical committee member might not recognise. Common examples include control panel, sensor, siren, zone, handover, commissioning and user testing. The committee does not need a technical definition of each term. It needs a sentence that says what the term means for the building and what the committee will see or receive.

A useful method is to read the quotation aloud and mark every word that would need a follow-up question if a neighbour asked about it. Those marked words become card headings. For each heading, write one plain question and one plain follow-up. For example, a card headed Zone might ask: What is a zone, and how many zones does this quotation include? The follow-up could be: Which parts of the building are in each zone? The answer space should be large enough for two or three sentences.

Avoid turning the cards into a technical checklist. The committee is not being asked to design the system. It is being asked to understand what it is approving. A card that asks for a wiring diagram or a device layout goes beyond the purpose of a quotation meeting and may not be answerable in that setting. Keep the language administrative: what is supplied, what is installed, what is tested, what is handed over, and what the committee must do next.

If the quotation includes a package similar to the published Hikvision Alarm System package, the card set can use the package wording as a starting point and then ask the supplier to explain any term that is not clear. The card should record the explanation in the supplier's own words, not in the committee's paraphrase.

## Sorting Administrative Questions Onto the Right Cards

Not every question belongs on the same card. A practical card set groups questions by the person who is likely to answer them. Commercial questions, such as what the price includes and what would change the price, belong on a card for the person presenting the quotation. Scheduling questions, such as when a site survey would happen and who from the premises team should be available, belong on a card for the project coordinator. Handover questions, such as what training is provided and what documents are issued, belong on a card for the person responsible for user testing and handover.

A simple three-card structure works for many small committees. Card one covers scope: what is included in the quoted price and what is excluded. Card two covers process: what happens before installation, during installation and at handover. Card three covers responsibilities: what the committee must prepare, who signs off, and how changes are recorded. Each card carries two or three questions, not ten.

The written answer should be captured on the card itself, with the responder's name and the date. If an answer is not available at the meeting, the card should record who will provide it and by when. This turns the card set into a small action log. The committee secretary can then type the answers into the meeting minutes without having to reconstruct the conversation from memory.

A site survey is a common example of a process question that needs a clear answer. Eyonic lists site survey as part of its service scope, so a card might ask when the survey would take place and what the committee should prepare for it. The card should not ask for a survey report at the quotation stage if the survey has not yet happened.

## Writing the Card Text So It Can Be Read Aloud

A question card is often read aloud in a meeting, so the text should survive being spoken. Short sentences help. One idea per sentence is better than a compound question with three parts. If a card asks two things at once, the responder may answer only the easier part.

Use the same words on the card that the committee uses in conversation. If the committee says alarm system rather than intrusion detection system, the card should say alarm system. If the quotation uses a technical term, the card can introduce it in brackets after the plain word, for example: the alarm panel (the control box for the system). This keeps the card readable while still connecting it to the quotation.

Leave a clear answer area. A card with a question at the top and a large blank space below is easier to use than a card with dense instructions. Include a line for the responder's name and a line for the date. If the answer is a number, such as the number of sensors included, provide a small box for the figure and a line for any qualification.

A card set should also include one card for questions the committee did not anticipate. Label it Other questions and leave it blank except for the answer lines. This gives the chair a place to record a question that arises during the meeting without disrupting the prepared cards. The goal is a complete written record, not a perfect script.

## Capturing the Written Answer and Closing the Loop

The value of the card set depends on what happens after the meeting. Each card should be collected, checked for a name and date, and stored with the quotation. The secretary should review the cards the same day and list any question that was not answered. That list becomes the follow-up agenda for the next conversation with the supplier.

A simple tracking table helps. Columns can include card number, question, answer given, responder, date, and whether the answer is complete. The committee can then see at a glance which parts of the quotation are clear and which need a written reply. If a supplier promises to send an answer later, the table should record the promised date and the person who made the promise.

When the answers are complete, the committee can attach the cards to the meeting minutes as an appendix. This is more useful than a narrative summary because it preserves the exact questions and answers. A future committee reviewing the same quotation can see what was asked and what was said, without relying on anyone's recollection.

Eyonic's published package for a Hikvision Alarm System includes user testing and handover, which suggests that handover is a distinct stage in the process. A card that asks what user testing involves and what the committee receives at handover can capture that stage in plain language. The answer should be written on the card, not left as a verbal promise.

## A Short Example Set for a Fictional Sarawak Committee

The following example is illustrative and is not a template for a real site. It shows how six cards might be worded for a fictional committee reviewing an ordinary alarm quotation in Sarawak.

Card 1, Scope: What exactly is included in the quoted price? Please list the main items. Card 2, Exclusions: What is not included that the committee might expect? Card 3, Site survey: When would a site survey take place, and who should be available? Card 4, Installation: What disruption should the committee expect during installation, and how long is the typical visit? Card 5, Handover: What user testing and handover will be provided, and what documents will the committee receive? Card 6, Changes: If the committee wants to change the scope after the quotation, how is that recorded and priced?

Each card has a blank space for the answer, a line for the responder's name, and a line for the date. The chair reads the card aloud, the responder answers, and the answer is written down before the next card is read. If an answer is not available, the card records who will follow up and by when.

The example set is deliberately short. A committee can add cards for its own priorities, but the six-card structure covers the administrative questions that most often cause confusion after a quotation meeting. The cards do not ask for device layout, sensor positions, credentials or operational details. Those belong to a later technical discussion, not to the quotation meeting.

A committee that is also discussing CCTV alongside the alarm quotation may wish to add a card asking how the two scopes are coordinated, but the card should still ask for a plain-language answer rather than a technical design.
