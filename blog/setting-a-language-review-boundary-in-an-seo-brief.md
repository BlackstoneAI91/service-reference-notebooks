## Why the Language Review Section Deserves Its Own Place in the Brief

An SEO brief usually carries keyword targets, page structure, internal links and a tone note. Language review often gets folded into a single line such as "check Malay version" and then disappears once writing starts. The result is predictable: an English title is approved, the Malay title is drafted later by someone else, and nobody can say who signed off the difference.

Giving language review its own section does three things. It names a person rather than a team. It records the exact strings under review, so a reviewer is not asked to approve a whole page when only the title and meta description are in question. And it separates two decisions that are easy to confuse: who edits the wording, and who approves a translation when the Malay label deliberately differs from the English one.

This matters most for service businesses whose customers search in either language. A page about a service may be read in English by one visitor and in Malay by another, and both titles need to describe the same offer without contradicting each other. The section is not a translation exercise for its own sake; it is a record of accountability for the words that appear in search results.

Keep the section short enough to be read. Two tables and a short approval line are usually enough. The value comes from the names and dates being filled in, not from the length of the instructions.

## The Worksheet: Fields to Capture for Each Language

A workable worksheet has one row per string under review. For each row, record the string ID, the page or template it belongs to, the English version, the Malay version, the reviewer for each language, the approval status, and the date. Add a column for notes, because the interesting cases are the ones where the two versions are not literal equivalents.

A second table handles the approval question. It lists any string where the Malay label differs in meaning or emphasis from the English one, names the person who approved that difference, and records the reason in one sentence. Reasons are useful later: a future editor can see whether a difference was a deliberate market choice or an oversight.

Two practical rules keep the worksheet honest. First, a reviewer should be named by role and person, not by department. Second, an approval should carry a date, because pages get edited and an approval from last year may no longer describe the current title.

## Worked Example: A Fictional Malaysian Services Firm

Consider a fictional firm, Cahaya Servis Sdn Bhd, offering air-conditioning servicing in two states. Its SEO brief covers a service page with an English title and a Malay title. The English title names the service and the town. The Malay title uses a shorter service label that the firm's counter staff use in conversation, and it drops the town to fit the display length.

The worksheet records the English string, the Malay string, the English reviewer (the content lead) and the Malay reviewer (a senior service adviser who speaks to customers daily). Because the Malay label is not a literal translation, the approval table adds a row: the service adviser approves the shorter label, with the note that customers use it in phone enquiries. The content lead countersigns so that the two titles are known to describe the same offer.

Nothing in this example depends on the firm's size. The point is that the difference was noticed, attributed and dated. A reader of the brief six months later can see why the Malay title is shorter without guessing.

Treat the example as a template, not a benchmark. Your own service labels will differ, and the reviewer who approves them should be the person closest to how customers actually speak.

## Writing a Neutral Handover Request

The handover request is the message that moves the worksheet from writer to reviewer. It should be neutral, specific and easy to decline in part. A useful pattern is: state the page, list the exact strings, say what decision is needed, give a deadline, and name the fallback if no decision arrives.

For example: "The service page brief is ready for language review. The English title and meta description are on rows 1 and 2; the Malay versions are on rows 3 and 4. Please confirm the Malay service label or propose an alternative. If I have no response by the stated date, the page will be held rather than published with an unapproved title."

Three habits make this work. Ask for a decision rather than an opinion, so the reviewer knows what to return. Keep the request to the strings under review, so it does not become a request to rewrite the page. And state the consequence of silence plainly, without pressure language.

## Before-Publication Checklist for the Language Review Section

A short checklist, run just before the page goes live, catches most of the gaps. Treat the items as prompts rather than a scored audit; the order below is a suggested sequence, not a researched standard.

One: every string in the worksheet has a named reviewer for each language. Two: every string where the Malay and English differ has an approval row with a reason. Three: the approval dates are current for this version of the page. Four: the English and Malay titles describe the same offer, even if the wording differs. Five: the reviewer who approved the translation is identifiable by role, not only by initials. Six: the handover request and its response are stored with the brief.

A seventh item is worth adding for teams that publish often: confirm that the person who approved the title is still the right approver. Staff move between roles, and an approval attached to a former job title is a quiet source of confusion.

If any item is unanswered, holding the page is usually cheaper than correcting a title after it has been shared. The checklist is deliberately small so that it gets used on ordinary pages, not only on major campaigns.

## Keeping the Section Useful After Publication

The language review section does not stop mattering once the page is live. Titles get edited, services get renamed, and a Malay label that suited one campaign may not suit the next. A brief that records who approved a translation gives the next editor a starting point instead of a blank page.

A simple habit is to date the section and note the version of the page it covers. When a title changes, add a new row rather than overwriting the old one. The history is short and readable, and it answers the question that otherwise takes an email thread to resolve: who decided this wording, and when.

It also helps to keep the review record with the content brief rather than in a separate folder. The brief is where the writer, the reviewer and the approver already look.
