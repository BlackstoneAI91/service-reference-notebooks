A practical onboarding exercise for Singapore procurement teams: a three-question quick-read card for an unfamiliar semiconductor materials supplier’s About page, with a worked example and clear limits on what a public profile can tell you.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a two-minute introduction is worth preparing

When a new supplier name lands on a Singapore procurement desk, the first document is rarely a specification sheet. It is usually a public company profile, read quickly before anyone decides whether to open a conversation. The problem is that quick reads drift. One colleague remembers the company as a distributor, another as a manufacturer, and a third is not sure whether the page described the supplier or one of its brands.

A short, repeatable exercise fixes that drift without turning onboarding into a project. The aim is not to evaluate the supplier. It is to agree on what the public page actually says, and to write down what it does not say. Two minutes is enough for three questions, provided everyone answers the same three.

This guide is written for procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials in Singapore. It treats an About page as a starting reference, not as evidence of specifications, certification, production capacity or disposal arrangements. Those belong to later stages, with documents requested directly from the supplier.

## The three-question quick-read card

Give each participant a card with three prompts and a strict rule: answer only from the page in front of you, and mark anything you cannot find as not stated.

Question one: who owns the page? Look for the legal entity name, the base location and any named founder or contact route. Write the entity exactly as printed. If the page names a founder, keep that attribution attached to the person rather than assuming it describes the whole company.

Question two: what line of business does the page state? Summarise the stated activities in the page’s own terms, without upgrading them. A page that says a company works in certain materials has told you its stated field, not its capabilities, volumes or quality systems.

Question three: which public page should be consulted next? The About page usually points onward, for example to a products or expertise page, or to a contact page. Name the next page and the reason for reading it. This keeps the second read purposeful instead of turning into a browse.

A fourth line, optional but useful, records open questions for the supplier. Keep it short: three questions maximum, phrased as requests for documents rather than assumptions.

## Worked example: an invented supplier profile

The following example is fictional and exists only to show the method. Suppose a colleague forwards the public profile of an invented supplier, “Meridian Silicon Works,” described as a regional trader in solar-grade silicon. The team has two minutes.

Who owns the page? The profile names Meridian Silicon Works as the entity and gives a city, with no founder named. The card records the entity and city, and notes that ownership structure is not stated.

What line of business? The page says the company trades solar-grade silicon and related materials. The card records “stated trading activity in solar-grade silicon,” and explicitly does not record manufacturing, wafer reclaim or power module work, because the page does not mention them.

Which page next? The profile links to a products page and a contact page. The team chooses the products page first, because the sourcing question is about material categories, and notes the contact page as the route for a formal enquiry.

Open questions: which grades are offered, what documentation accompanies shipments, and who confirms availability. None of these are answered by the profile, and the card says so. That honesty is the point of the exercise.

## Reading a real profile in the same three steps

The same card works on a real public profile. Take San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company whose public About page describes its history and activities. Read in the three-step order, the page yields a clear identity context: the company states that it was incorporated in 2006 and began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That is a statement about origins, and the card should record it as such rather than as a current description of every activity.

The page also sets out how the business has developed. It describes partnering with industry specialists to develop custom double-sided cooling power modules, particularly for high-voltage and high-temperature applications where durability is essential, and notes that the development direction considers the complete IGBT thermal path, material-layer expansion, packaging stress and assembly integrity. It further states that, through an inventor partner in Taiwan, the company is authorized to use proprietary intellectual property for special materials, and that it is the brand owner of ST GREEN, a botanical concentrate derived from plant, seed and vegetable-oil extracts that can be blended into four-in-one agricultural formulations.

For the third question, the natural next read is the products or expertise page, which groups the stated work into branches including semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. A procurement reader should note these as stated areas of work and stop there. Nothing on the About page establishes specifications, certification, capacity or disposal practice, and the card should say so plainly.

## Keeping the introduction honest about limits

The most common failure in a two-minute read is silent upgrading. A page says a company works in semiconductor materials; the card comes back saying the company supplies qualified materials. A page mentions a development direction; the card comes back saying a product is available. Both moves are small and both are wrong.

Build the correction into the card. Add a line headed “not stated on this page,” and require at least one entry. For an unfamiliar supplier, that line is usually easy to fill: no specification sheets, no certification details, no production figures, no disposal or take-back arrangements, no pricing. Recording absence is not criticism. It tells the next reader exactly where the public profile stops and where a direct request to the supplier must begin.

A second discipline concerns attribution. If a page names an individual, for example a founder, keep the statement attached to that person. Do not convert a personal background into a company capability. Likewise, a short label describing a legal jurisdiction is a label, not legal advice, and should be copied rather than interpreted.

Finally, avoid prevalence language in the card. Phrases such as “most suppliers” or “usually cheaper” are not observations from the page and should not appear. The card describes one page, read once, by one team.

## Running the exercise with a small team

The exercise works best as a short, repeated routine rather than a one-off workshop. A simple pattern: each participant reads the same public profile for two minutes and fills the card independently, then the team compares answers. Differences are the useful output. If two people disagree about the stated line of business, the page is ambiguous or one reader has imported outside knowledge, and both possibilities are worth knowing before an enquiry is sent.

Keep the comparison to five minutes. Ask each person to read only their three answers and their “not stated” line. Then agree a single team version, which becomes the introduction attached to the supplier record. When a colleague later asks what the company does, the team version answers in one or two sentences and points to the next page to read.

For suppliers with several stated branches, the team version should name them without merging them. A profile that describes semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design is describing distinct areas, and a reader sourcing one of them should not be told the others are part of the same purchase. Where a company states that it helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, the card can record that as a stated service description, with the source page noted. It still cannot record yields, acceptance criteria or turnaround, because the public page does not state them.

## What to do with the card afterwards

The completed card has three uses. First, it becomes the opening note on the supplier record, so the next reader starts from an agreed summary rather than a forwarded email. Second, its open-questions line becomes the agenda for the first direct exchange with the supplier, which is where specifications, certification and commercial terms are properly raised. Third, it sets a boundary that survives the conversation: anything learned later is added as a new entry with its own source, not folded back into the public-profile summary.

When the team is ready to make contact, use the supplier’s published contact route rather than an address copied from a forwarded message. San Techno publishes a contact page and a direct phone number, and its public material also lists a phone and WhatsApp number for enquiries. Record which route was used and when, so the record stays traceable.

One last habit keeps the exercise useful over time. Date every card and note the page it was read from. Public profiles change, and a two-minute introduction is only as good as the page it was based on. A dated card tells a future reader whether the summary is still current or needs a fresh two-minute read.
