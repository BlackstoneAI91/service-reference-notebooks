## Two notes, one bag, two different jobs

A fictional host in Sarawak runs a two-room short-stay unit near a riverfront. On a Friday the laundry comes back and two pieces of paper sit on top of the folded linen. Both are short. Both mention the same booking. Both are signed off with a name and a time. Read quickly, they look like twins.

They are not. One is a record: it says what came back, in what state, and who checked it. The other is a request: it describes something the writer wants the host to confirm, correct or decide. The problem is that neither note says which one it is. The host files both, and the request quietly goes unanswered until the next turnover.

This guide is about that gap. It gives you two labels to separate the notes by purpose, a short routine for deciding which one needs a reply, and a way to write your own return notes so the next reader does not have to guess. ## Label one: the Filed Record

A Filed Record is a note whose only job is to be kept. It answers: what came back, when, and in what condition at the point of return. It does not ask the reader to do anything. If you never open it again, nothing breaks.

Typical contents of a Filed Record:

- A reference to the booking or room it relates to.
- A count or list of what was returned.
- A short condition note, such as a mark noticed on inspection.
- The name of the person who checked it and the date.

A Filed Record can still contain bad news. A stain noted on a pillowcase is a record, not a request, unless the writer asks you to decide something about it. The test is simple: does the note contain a question, a choice, or an instruction aimed at you? If not, it is a record.

Filed Records are worth keeping because they give you a baseline. When a guest disputes a mark, or when you want to compare one return against the last, the record is your reference point. File it by date and by room, not by the mood of the day.

One caution: a Filed Record is not evidence of a supplier's fault or of your own diligence. It is only a note of what someone saw at one moment. Treat it as a memory aid, not a verdict.

## Label two: the Reply Request

A Reply Request is a note that expects something back from you. It may be polite enough to sound like a record. It may even begin with the same words. But somewhere inside it there is a question, a choice, or a request for confirmation, and the writer is waiting.

Common disguises of a Reply Request:

- A statement phrased as fact that actually needs your agreement: "Item set aside pending your instruction."
- A soft question buried mid-paragraph: "Let us know if the count is right."
- A choice offered without a deadline: "We can return it as is or hold it."
- A condition note that implies a decision: "Mark noted on the duvet cover; advise."

The danger is not that these notes are unclear in themselves. It is that they sit next to a Filed Record with the same shape, same length and same tone. The reader's eye skips both.

A useful habit is to read every return note once asking a single question: is anyone waiting on me? If the answer is yes, the note is a Reply Request regardless of how it is worded. If the answer is no, it is a Filed Record. The label follows the purpose, not the format.

## A short triage routine for the return pile

You do not need a system to separate two notes. You need a routine you will actually repeat. Here is one example you can adapt; treat the timings as author heuristics, not researched standards.

Step one: sort before you read. Put every note from the return into one pile. Do not file anything until the pile is empty.

Step two: read for the question. For each note, ask: is anyone waiting on me? Mark the ones where the answer is yes. A highlighter or a folded corner is enough.

Step three: answer the marked notes first. Even a one-line reply that says you have seen the note and will decide later closes the loop. Silence does not.

Step four: file the rest. Filed Records go into your date-and-room file. Reply Requests go into a small open-items list until they are answered, then into the same file with your reply attached.

Step five: review the open list before the next turnover. If a Reply Request is still open, it is now a risk to your next guest, not just an unanswered note.

A five-minute sort at the end of a return is a reasonable example target for a small unit. Larger operations may need a named person to own the open list. The point is not speed; it is that no request is left sitting under a record.

## Writing return notes that label themselves

The cleanest fix is upstream: write your own return notes so the reader never has to guess. Two labels, printed or written at the top, do most of the work.

Use Filed Record at the top of any note that only reports. Keep it to what came back, when, and in what condition. No questions, no choices, no instructions.

Use Reply Request at the top of any note that needs an answer. Then make the ask explicit: what you need, from whom, and by when. A Reply Request without a named ask is just a record with anxiety.

A short template for a Reply Request:

- Label: Reply Request.
- Reference: booking or room.
- The ask: one sentence, in the imperative.
- The decision needed: what you want the reader to choose or confirm.
- The date you need it by.

A short template for a Filed Record:

- Label: Filed Record.
- Reference: booking or room.
- What returned, and the condition noted at inspection.
- Who checked it, and when.

## Where the two labels meet specialist cleaning

Short-stay units rarely send only linen. A turnover may also involve a marked rug, a set of curtains, a guest's formal outfit, a pair of shoes left behind, or a soft toy a child has carried through the unit. Each of those can generate its own return note, and each note can be either a record or a request.

A few examples of how the labels apply:

- A note saying a small rug was returned after carpet cleaning with a condition remark is a Filed Record unless it asks you to decide something.
- A note saying curtains were returned and asking whether you want them rehung is a Reply Request, because it offers a choice.
- A note about a wedding gown cleaning return that lists the pieces checked is a Filed Record; one that asks you to confirm a mark is a Reply Request.
- A note about shoe cleaning that reports the pairs returned is a Filed Record; one that asks whether to proceed with a second pair is a Reply Request.
- A note about soft toy cleaning that reports the items returned is a Filed Record; one that asks you to confirm a child's toy is the right one is a Reply Request.

## A worked example: the fictional riverfront unit

Return to the fictional host near the riverfront. Two notes arrive on Friday.

Note A reads: "Two sets of bed linen and four towels returned. One pillowcase has a light mark noted at inspection. Checked by A., 3pm." No question. This is a Filed Record. It goes into the date-and-room file. The mark is noted, not decided.

Note B reads: "Two sets of bed linen and four towels returned. One pillowcase has a light mark noted at inspection. We can hold the piece for your instruction or return it as is. Checked by A., 3pm." Same opening, same count, same mark. But the middle sentence offers a choice. This is a Reply Request. It needs an answer, even if the answer is "return as is, I will decide later."

Under the old habit, both notes were filed and the choice in Note B was never made. Under the two-label habit, Note B goes onto the open-items list, gets a one-line reply, and is filed with the reply attached. The next turnover starts with no hidden decisions.

That is the whole method. The notes still look alike. You just stop treating them alike.

## Keeping the habit without over-engineering it

Two labels and a five-step sort are enough for most small operations. Resist the urge to build a form for everything. The habit works because it is light enough to repeat on a tired Friday evening.

A few practical notes:

- Keep the labels visible. Write them at the top of every note, even internal ones.
- Do not let a Filed Record contain a question. If you catch one, relabel it.
- Do not let a Reply Request go out without a named ask and a date.
- Review the open list before each turnover, not after a complaint.
- When a formal process exists in your written arrangements, follow it; your labels are for the informal notes around it.
