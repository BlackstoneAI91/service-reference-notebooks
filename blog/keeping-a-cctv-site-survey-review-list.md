## Why a review list matters more than a tidy draft

A site-survey draft for CCTV security systems is rarely read by one person. It moves from a premises manager to a tenant representative, to a reception lead, and then to an authorised installer. Each reader sees a different part of the building and a different part of the business need. Without a list, the manager remembers that "someone looked at it" but cannot say which version, which questions were answered, or who agreed to the final wording.

The purpose of the list is not surveillance. It is bookkeeping. You are recording who was asked, what they were asked to check, and whether their comments were folded into the next version. That record protects the project from quiet drift, where a draft is edited three times and nobody can say which copy the installer is working from.

Keep the list deliberately thin. Role labels, version numbers, dates and open questions are enough. Do not paste layouts, camera positions, credentials or recording details into a shared review log. The log should be safe to forward to a tenant representative who only needs to confirm that their floor is mentioned.

## Start with a non-sensitive consultation draft

Before anyone is asked to review, decide what the draft is allowed to contain. A consultation draft is a business document, not an installation drawing. It can describe the rooms and entrances that need attention, the people who move through them, and the questions the premises manager still needs answered. It should not carry cable routes, device settings, access credentials or a final camera schedule.

A useful structure for the draft is four short parts: the spaces in scope, the daily activities in those spaces, the open questions, and the decisions needed from named roles. That structure keeps the conversation at the level of business requirements, which is where tenant representatives and reception staff can actually contribute.

This is also the point to note that a CCTV site survey is a normal part of planning. That context helps reviewers understand that the draft is an early planning document, not a final design.

## Name the roles, not the people

A review register works best when it uses role labels. People change desks, go on leave and hand over. Roles persist. Use labels such as tenant representative, reception lead, facilities coordinator, authorised installer and approval owner. Add a column for the date a copy was sent and the date a response came back.

One row per role per version is enough. If the reception lead reviews version two and then version three, that is two rows, not one. The version number is the anchor that stops the conversation from sliding back to an older copy.

Keep the register free of personal commentary. "Reception lead: asked whether the visitor waiting area is described correctly" is useful. "Reception lead: unhappy" is not, because it cannot be acted on and it invites dispute. The register is a record of questions and answers, not a place to store opinions about colleagues.

Where a role has not responded, leave the row open and visible. An unanswered row is more honest than a blank space that everyone assumes was handled.

## A sample review register you can adapt

The following is an example layout, not a template taken from any live project. Adapt the columns to your own building.

Columns: Version, Date circulated, Role, What they were asked to check, Response received, Open question carried forward, Approval owner.

Example rows, using role labels only:

Version 1, circulated to tenant representative. Asked to confirm that the shared entrance and the document collection point are described accurately. Response received. One open question about after-hours access carried forward.

Version 1, circulated to reception lead. Asked to confirm that the visitor waiting area and the staff counter are named correctly. No response yet. Row left open.

Version 2, circulated to facilities coordinator. Asked to confirm that the service corridor is in scope. Response received. No open questions.

Version 2, circulated to authorised installer. Asked to confirm that the draft lists the business requirements the installer needs before any site visit. Response received. Two questions returned to the premises manager.

Version 3, circulated to approval owner. Asked to confirm that all open questions are either answered or explicitly deferred. Approval owner named in the final column.

The register stays short because each row answers one question: did this role see this version, and what came back? If a row needs more than a sentence, the detail belongs in the draft, not the log.

## Keep one accountable approval owner

A review list without an approval owner becomes a discussion group. Name one role, not a committee, as the person who confirms that a version is ready to move forward. That role does not have to answer every technical question. It has to confirm that the open questions are visible and that nothing has been quietly dropped.

The approval owner should sign off on the version number, not on the whole project. "Version 3 approved for the next planning step" is a clear statement. "CCTV approved" is not, because it implies a finality the draft does not have.

When the draft moves toward installation, the approval owner is also the person who confirms that the installer has the current version. This is where a clear handover matters. Eyonic Security Systems describes its CCTV work as planning, supplying and installing monitoring systems with camera placement, recording and remote viewing. A review register that names the version handed to the installer keeps that planning conversation anchored to one document.

## Log unanswered business requirements separately

Some questions will not be answered during the review. A tenant representative may need to check with a colleague. A reception lead may be unsure whether a side door is used after hours. Do not let those questions disappear into email threads.

Keep a short open-questions list beside the register. Each item gets a number, the role that raised it, the role that owns the answer, and a target date. When the question is answered, mark it closed and note which version incorporated the answer. When it is deferred, say so explicitly and record who agreed to defer it.

This list is where business requirements live. It is not the place for camera operating steps, wiring details or recording settings. Those belong with the installer and the technical planning conversation, not in a shared review log that tenant representatives can read.

A simple rule helps: if a question can be answered by someone who does not work with CCTV equipment, it belongs on the open-questions list. If it cannot, it belongs in a separate technical discussion with the authorised installer.

## Where related decisions fit without cluttering the log

A premises review often touches more than cameras. Door access may come up, especially where a reception or staff entrance is being discussed. Keep those topics in their own short note rather than mixing them into the CCTV review register.

Eyonic Security Systems notes that smart locks work best when the door condition, user roles and backup method are reviewed before installation, and that for business use it usually checks whether a standalone smart lock or an access-control panel is more appropriate. That is a useful reminder that door access is a separate decision with its own review questions. If a reviewer raises it, log it as a related item and route it to the right discussion rather than folding it into the camera draft.

Similarly, if the conversation turns to what happens after installation, note the handover expectations as a separate item. Eyonic Security Systems lists remote viewing among its services, and its published package information mentions mobile viewing handover as part of a basic CCTV package. Treat that as brand context for what a handover conversation may cover, not as a promise about any specific project.

## A short closing checklist for the premises manager

Before you circulate the next version, run through these checks. They are author heuristics, not an audited standard.

One: the draft contains no layouts, credentials, wiring details or recording settings. Two: every role on the register has a row for the current version. Three: every open question has an owner and a target date. Four: one approval owner is named and has confirmed the version number. Five: the installer has been told which version is current. Six: related topics such as door access or handover are logged separately and not mixed into the camera draft.

If you can answer all six, the register is doing its job. It shows who reviewed what, which questions remain open, and who is accountable for the next step. That is a clearer position than a folder of edited drafts with no record of who saw which one.

For premises managers in Sarawak planning a CCTV installation, the review register is a small piece of administration that keeps the planning conversation honest. It does not replace a site survey or a technical discussion with an installer. It simply makes sure that when those conversations happen, everyone is looking at the same version and the same list of open questions.
